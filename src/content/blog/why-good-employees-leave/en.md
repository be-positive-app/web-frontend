Good people rarely announce they are unhappy. They do not slam the door. They go quiet, and one Monday there is a resignation letter on your desk that "came out of nowhere".

It almost never comes out of nowhere. The signals were there, in data you already have. Here are six that show up first.

## 1. They stopped taking time off

Someone who has not taken a day off for many months is not always dedicated. Often they are running on empty. Burnout looks like loyalty until it doesn't.

## 2. Their mood check-ins dropped

If your team marks how the day went, a person whose answers slide from "good" to "so-so" over a few weeks is telling you something they will not say out loud.

## 3. They have not been thanked in a long time

Recognition is the cheapest retention tool there is, and the most forgotten. When was the last time anyone said "thank you" to this person, in writing, where others could see it?

## 4. Their skills stopped growing

People leave jobs where they stopped learning. If someone's skill levels have not moved in a year, they have probably noticed before you.

## 5. Late work piles up

A sudden rise in overdue tasks from a reliable person is rarely laziness. It is overload, a problem at home, or a decision already half made.

## 6. They are new

The first months are the riskiest. A new hire who has not been checked on, thanked or given a clear first goal is the easiest one to lose.

## What to do this week

Pick the two people who show the most of these signals and have a ten-minute conversation. Do not ask "are you happy?". Ask what would make next month better, and what is getting in the way. Then thank someone in public.
